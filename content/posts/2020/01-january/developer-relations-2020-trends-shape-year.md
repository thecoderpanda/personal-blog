Not too long ago, if you asked a tech executive what "Developer Relations" (DevRel) was, they’d probably describe a highly caffeinated engineer who spent 300 days a year flying to tech conferences, handing out branded t-shirts, ordering late-night hackathon pizzas, and showing college students how to send an SMS using their API. 

It was a niche role, often treated as a sub-department of marketing with a vague mandate: "Make developers like us." 

Well, it is 2020, and the tech industry is waking up to a cold, hard reality: **developers are the new kingmakers of enterprise software.** 

We are living in an API-driven, developer-first economy. Companies like Stripe, Twilio, SendGrid, and Plaid have proven that if you win the hearts, minds, and terminal consoles of software engineers, you build an unstoppable, multi-billion-dollar business. 

Because of this, DevRel is shedding its reputation as a feel-good marketing sideline. It is growing up, turning highly technical, and becoming a core strategic business function. 

Let's take a look at the major DevRel trends that are going to shape 2020, and why companies that neglect developer experience (DX) are about to get left in the dust.

---

## 1. The Developer-First Purchasing Shift

In the traditional enterprise sales model, software was sold via golf games and expensive steak dinners. A sales rep from Oracle or IBM would take a CIO out to lunch, pitch them a proprietary system, and sign a seven-figure contract. The software would then be shoved down the throats of the developers, who spent years fighting with bad docs and terrible integration interfaces.

In 2020, that top-down model is dead. 

The modern software purchase starts at the bottom. When a team needs to solve a problem—say, setting up user authentication or integrating credit card payments—they don't wait for a sales meeting. They search Google, read documentation, copy a quickstart script, and try to get a working prototype in fifteen minutes. 

If the developer experience is seamless, they show it to their team lead, who plugs in a corporate credit card. 

The developer is the ultimate gatekeeper. If your documentation is outdated, your SDKs are clunky, or your API takes more than three steps to return a `200 OK` status, the developer will close their browser tab and find a competitor. In 2020, DevRel is the department tasked with ensuring that when that developer hits your landing page, they find an absolute masterpiece of developer experience.

---

## 2. The Evolution from "Evangelism" to "Developer Experience Engineering"

Let's talk about the word "Developer Evangelist." 

Frankly, engineers are incredibly skeptical people. They can smell marketing fluff and corporate sales pitches from a mile away. If a DevRel representative stands on a stage and pitches a product, but can’t explain how it handles high-concurrency database queries, explain its underlying security model, or help debug a complex multi-threaded error, they lose all credibility.

In 2020, we are seeing the decline of the pure "evangelist" and the rise of the **Developer Experience (DX) Engineer**. 

These are highly skilled software engineers whose primary language is code, not slides. Their responsibilities go far beyond writing blog posts and speaking at meetups. They are actively:
*   **Building robust SDKs** in multiple programming languages.
*   **Creating real-world, production-ready sample apps** (no more trivial "Hello World" tutorials; we want full-stack, deployed GitHub repos).
*   **Writing interactive, self-updating documentation** that allows developers to test API requests directly in the browser.
*   **Contributing directly to the core product codebase** based on the real-world feedback they gather from community developers.

DevRel is no longer just a megaphone to talk *to* developers; it is the critical, bidirectional bridge that brings developer frustrations back to the internal product teams.

---

## 3. Web3 and Crypto: DevRel on Hard Mode

While Web2 companies are still optimizing their developer funnels, the Web3 and blockchain sectors are rewriting the DevRel playbook entirely. 

In the decentralized space, developer relations isn't just important—it is literally a matter of survival. 

If you build a new Layer 1 blockchain or a DeFi protocol, your project is a ghost town if external developers don't build on top of it. Without developers deploying dApps, writing integrations, and building tools, your protocol’s token is just a speculative bubble waiting to burst.

Crypto DevRel is DevRel on hard mode. It requires:
*   **Open-source facilitation**: Managing decentralization, where contributors are spread across the globe and aren't employees of your company.
*   **Extreme technical depth**: Explaining complex cryptography, zero-knowledge proofs, and EVM (Ethereum Virtual Machine) mechanics.
*   **High-stakes debugging**: Helping developers write secure code where a single compiler mistake can result in millions of dollars of user funds being permanently locked or drained.

In 2020, the projects that win the blockchain space will be those that build the best developer ecosystems. Expect to see massive capital allocations toward developer grants, global hackathons, and high-quality educational initiatives.

---

## 4. The Rise of the DevRel Analytics Stack

In the past, DevRel was notoriously difficult to measure. Advocates would tell their executives: *"We had great engagement at the conference! We gave away 500 stickers!"* 

That doesn't cut it in a modern, metrics-driven company. In 2020, successful DevRel leaders are defining clear, analytical funnels that mirror traditional product-led growth (PLG) metrics.

We are looking at:
*   **Time to First Hello World (TTFHW)**: The exact number of seconds it takes a developer to sign up, read the docs, make their first API call, and get a successful response. This is the single most important DX metric.
*   **SDK Adoption and Retention**: Tracking package downloads (NPM, PyPI, Maven) and seeing if developers continue to use them over time.
*   **GitHub Activity**: Monitoring the velocity of issues opened, PRs submitted, and the response time of your team to community requests.
*   **Developer Activation Cost (DAC)**: Measuring the efficiency of hackathons, developer events, and tutorials in converting anonymous visitors into active API consumers.

By applying data science to developer relations, DevRel teams can finally prove their direct contribution to customer acquisition and revenue growth, securing their seat at the executive table.

---

## Conclusion: DX is a Product Feature

If your company's product is built for developers, you need to understand that **Developer Experience is not an afterthought.** It is a core feature of your product. 

You can have the most advanced, high-performance database or payment infrastructure in the world, but if your API is a pain to integrate, a developer will choose a slower, less advanced competitor with a beautiful, three-line setup.

As we move deeper into 2020, the companies that thrive will be those that treat developers with the utmost respect, provide them with world-class tools, and fund highly technical, empathetic DevRel teams. 

Developer relations has officially grown up. It’s time to stop measuring stickers, and start measuring code.
